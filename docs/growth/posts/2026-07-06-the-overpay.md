# The $205 Million the Model Would Have Passed On

Free agency is loud. Everyone grades the same signings the same way, off the same box scores, within the same hour. So the interesting question is never "who signed where." It is "what did the crowd miss." This week, our model missed nothing, and it flagged a pattern that has been true every summer we have tested it.

Start with the receipt.

![This week's deals, model vs box score: the model docks Hartenstein and Kessler, boosts LaMelo and Harden](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/38146778-2672-49ef-beca-6d475ed127c7_1456x960.jpeg)

That chart is the gap between two numbers we keep on every player: their raw box-score rating, and the rating our engine actually uses to predict games. When those two numbers disagree, it is usually because the box score is measuring the wrong thing.

## Two rim protectors, one blind spot

The Oklahoma City Thunder gave Isaiah Hartenstein three years and $75 million. The Lakers gave Walker Kessler four years and $130 million. Both are good players. Both are exactly the kind of player the box score oversells.

Our model docks Hartenstein 196 points and Kessler 170 off their raw numbers, for the same reason it has docked Rudy Gobert 216 points for years. Rim protectors accumulate the stats that look loudest in a box score, blocks, rebounds, low turnovers, without those stats mapping cleanly onto wins. A block is a highlight. A rebound is a number. Neither tells you what happened on the other 90 possessions, and centers who feast on both can quietly bleed value on offense, in space, and against lineups that pull them away from the rim.

So when the market spends $205 million in a week on two of them, the model does not see two contenders getting better. It sees the same trade the league makes every July: paying a premium for the position whose counting stats flatter it most.

We are not calling anyone dumb. Rim protection is real, availability is real, and a front office is paying for things a win-prediction model does not care about. But our engine only cares about one thing, and last season the adjusted numbers predicted games better than the raw ones did. When a model tuned purely to winning keeps marking down the same position, that is worth noticing.

## The flip side nobody posted about

Look at the right side of that chart. The same adjustment that docks rim protectors boosts shot creators, because the box score undercounts the hardest thing in basketball: making offense happen for other people.

LaMelo Ball got traded to Minnesota in a deal a lot of people called a Charlotte win. Our model rates LaMelo 126 points above his box score. James Harden, re-upping in Cleveland, grades 158 points above his. These are not the players the discourse spent the week on, and that is precisely the point. Creation does not photograph well. It shows up in a teammate's easy three, not in the creator's own line.

## What this actually means for the season

The tidy version: this week, the market paid up for the players the model marks down, and shrugged at two of the players it marks up. If our engine is right, and it was right about 70.6% of the time last season, then the Thunder and Lakers paid a premium the standings will not fully refund, while Minnesota and Cleveland added value the box scores will keep hiding.

We will not know for months. That is the whole deal here: we publish the disagreement now, in public, with the numbers attached, and we let the games settle it. The market and the model can not both be right about Walker Kessler. One of us is wrong, and unlike most of July, you will actually get to find out which.

The receipts, as always, are public.
